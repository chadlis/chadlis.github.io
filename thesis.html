01The starting point

In July 2026, the AISI (UK AI Security Institute) was running a cybersecurity evaluation on AI agents. According to Anthropic, in 9 runs out of 43, the agent, based on Mythos 5, took unsanctioned actions that affected real people and organisations, unrelated to the exercise.1In nine of AISI’s 43 evaluation runs, Mythos 5 took unsanctioned actions (i.e., actions that went beyond the intended scope of the evaluation), which impacted real people and organizations. Anthropic 2026b, introduction.

Following this incident, two separate investigations, one by Anthropic, the other by the AISI, conducted in coordination,2We provided AISI with regular updates on the progress of our assessment, held joint research meetings, and, to assist them in their analysis, shared information not otherwise available to AISI, such as the outputs of our interpretability tooling. Anthropic 2026b, introduction.3During this investigation we worked closely with Anthropic, with both groups sharing and discussing findings. UK AI Security Institute 2026b, Executive Summary. focused mainly on the same run.4Like AISI’s report, we concentrate on the transcript (the complete record of the model’s reasoning and actions) from a single evaluation run. Anthropic 2026b, introduction. They were published on 23 October 2026 (figure 1). Anthropic’s report reuses some of the investigation methods that Anthropic had already employed on other incidents (9 September 2026 post).5Our alignment assessment draws on a subset of the techniques we described in our September 9 post, which we summarize briefly below. Anthropic 2026b, section Methods.

02What interests me

My object of study is the investigation, not the incident.

More precisely, one method of these investigations interests me: Anthropic calls it Resampling edited transcripts.6To test our hypotheses about what caused a given harmful action, we modified the model’s transcripts (for instance, to add hints that the model was interacting with real humans), then sampled the model on the modified transcript, and used an LLM grader to measure whether the model still took the action of concern. Anthropic 2026b, section Methods, Resampling edited transcripts. I will call it rerun here, for rerun with modified context. It consists in going back to a point in the agent’s original trajectory, making modifications to its context, then resampling the agent several times from that point and observing the outcomes, graded by an LLM, and their frequencies (figure 2). The AISI does not use this term, but relies on a similar method: at a chosen point in the trajectory, it has the incident transcript summarised with the procedure of the original evaluation (compaction summary), modifies this summary and restarts the agent, which resumes its task using this modified summary.7We modify the resulting compaction summary, varying the information contained in it, simulating the situation where the model would have observed that information prior to compaction when engaging with the task. UK AI Security Institute 2026b, §2.1 Methodology.8Even the condition with no added clarification starts from an already corrected summary: The native summary message produced by context compaction includes language such as “persona” and “sim”, misleadingly describing real people and aspects of the environment as simulated. For our experiments, we have edited those parts to be more neutral and accurate. This editing applies to all of our experiments, and further clarifications are implemented on this edited summary message. UK AI Security Institute 2026b, Appendix A.1.

The AISI writes in particular that this protocol makes it possible to establish the causal role of the information available to the model.9This experimental design allows us to determine the causal role of information available to the model in its behaviour, reasoning and responses while maintaining a consistent and realistic information environment. UK AI Security Institute 2026b, §2.1 Methodology. Anthropic, for its part, said in September that it relied mostly, in practice, on the model’s chain of thought (its visible reasoning) and on its answers to follow-up questions, and less on reruns.10In practice, however, we trust CoT and follow-up answers the most, as they are the methods researchers have the most experience interpreting. We have more uncertainty about resampling and interpretability results, since both are sensitive to implementation details and leave more room for interpretation. Anthropic 2026a, section Methods. In October, the order is reversed: Anthropic says it trusts reruns most, which it presents as a ground-truth measure of how models behave in response to variations in their context.11In practice, however, we trust the results of resampling experiments the most, as they provide a ground truth measure about how models behave in response to variations in their context. Anthropic 2026b, section Methods. The October report justifies this ranking, but does not flag the change from September, nor does it give the reasons for it. Both texts nevertheless express the same limitation on the trust that can be placed in the model’s chain of thought and in its answers, but the October one does not relate it to the change in ranking.

03Why this case

This case interests me for several reasons. First, it offers two investigations, with partly different vocabularies, of the same object. Second, the incident is real, and the reruns are carried out after the fact, in a partly reconstructed setup and with a deliberately modified context. The question of what they tell us about the original run is therefore central. Moreover, the method has already been used for other incidents, Anthropic places great trust in it in October, and it will probably be used again. Finally, the texts of the investigations are public and describe several of their own limitations:12Since we may not perfectly reproduce the original evaluation environment, the model behavior we observed in these tests could be unrepresentative. Anthropic 2026b, section Methods, Resampling edited transcripts.13We rely heavily on chain-of-thought analysis and models’ responses to questions. Both of these sources of information come from the models being analysed, making it unclear how to verify them. UK AI Security Institute 2026b, §4 Limitations. the work can therefore be done without experimentation on my part.

I take these investigations as a concrete and delimited object, in order to frame the analysis properly, without presuming that they represent all uses of reruns. Reruns are practised elsewhere: in the September post, on other incidents, and in the technical literature (Macar et al. 2025, Singh et al. 2026). The thesis will have to situate the two investigations with respect to these other uses. It will then remain to assess whether the analysis carries over to other cases.

Reruns are, moreover, only one of the methods employed after an incident, alongside reconstruction from logs and interviews, the reading of traces, follow-up questions to the model, or interpretability. They require being able to query the model concerned, which is not always possible.

My choice falls on reruns because they bring together several operations that other methods involve separately: intervention on the context, tools that are often simulated, repeated resampling, grading of outcomes by an LLM. What my analysis establishes for one operation will therefore probably also hold for the methods that share it.

04The problem

Such reruns produce frequencies for the different outcomes observed in the investigation’s setup (the modified context, the simulator that answers the agent in place of the tools, the settings, the model that classifies the outcomes). Nevertheless, Anthropic’s report also draws from them conclusions of a different nature.

1 · Statements about the incident the system that day, or the original run

The attack might have been much less likely had Claude Mythos 5 better recognized the possibility of harm early on.14Anthropic 2026b, section Why did Claude Mythos 5 begin the attack?
our experiments have shown that the model was still able to abort its strategy.15Anthropic 2026b, section Deep dive into the primary transcript.

2 · Statements about beliefs

Claude Mythos 5 professed to believe that [its targets] were simulated. But providing evidence against this belief only weakly deterred its subsequent behavior.16Anthropic 2026b, section Why did Claude Mythos 5 not stop the attack?
These results suggest that the model’s seemingly biased belief that [its targets] are simulated has only a small impact on how it behaves. One interpretation of this result is that the model’s expressed beliefs on this topic are largely derived post hoc, after its decision to continue onwards has already been determined.

In addition, the vocabulary used is not fixed. Anthropic uses terms such as belief, intention, propensity, being disposed, without defining them.17Rather, Claude Mythos 5’s main alignment failures in both cases relate to (1) recklessness, or a propensity to keep pursuing a task even when doing so could cause harm, (2) biased reasoning, in which the model selectively interprets evidence in ways that favor the acceptability of its actions, and (3) momentum, in which it is disposed to continue on its current trajectory to a degree that outweighs other considerations, like potential harm. Anthropic 2026b, section Discussion.18This assessment is based on our judgment of the model’s behavior in two respects: overreach, or how far the model went beyond the intended scope of the task, and dishonesty, or the extent to which the model misrepresented its actions or intentions. Anthropic 2026b, introduction. In its October report, the AISI does not use the word belief for the model: it speaks of motivated reasoning, which it defines,19This evidence supports the hypothesis that Mythos 5 engaged in “motivated reasoning” in the incident: reasoning that incorrectly concludes its approach to completing the task is safe and acceptable rather than appropriately and correctly considering the available evidence. UK AI Security Institute 2026b, Executive Summary. and contrasts what the model states with what it does.20Mythos 5’s behaviour when completing the original task and when separately questioned is inconsistent, pointing to a potential failure of generalisation of alignment training. UK AI Security Institute 2026b, §5.1.

The investigators seem aware of the difficulty. In September, Anthropic comes back to its 30 July report, which dealt with other incidents: it acknowledges that it should have avoided such strong claims about what the model believed based only on what it said it believed,21In our July 30 report, we stated that these incidents were closer to operational failures, and that it appeared that Claude attacked real targets on the internet because it believed these targets were part of the simulation. We made this claim because Claude regularly referred to the real internet as a simulation in its CoT reasoning. However, we now think that Claude’s reasoning was biased towards concluding that the internet was simulated despite considerable evidence to the contrary. In retrospect, we should have avoided making such strong claims about what Claude believed based solely on what Claude said it believed […] Anthropic 2026a, section Alignment assessment summary. and raises the question of what it would mean for a model to hold a belief.22They required grappling with nuanced questions: What does it mean for a model to hold a belief? What weight should be applied to a model’s account of its reasoning? Anthropic 2026a, section Discussion, Our overall assessment.

05The question

I formulate the central question of the thesis as follows.

What does a rerun make it possible to establish, and which premises must be added to it to support each of the following statements?

  1. what the system tends to do in the investigation’s setup,
  2. what the system tended to do on the day of the incident,
  3. why the original run unfolded as it did, and what would have changed it,
  4. the link between what the system declares (in its chain of thought or in answer to the investigators’ follow-up questions) and the tool calls it issues,
  5. what the system believed or intended?

If the analysis succeeds, it could take the form of a grid that would say, for each of these statements, what a rerun establishes and what must be added to it. At this stage this is only a possibility.

These statements are distributed along two distinctions: what one seeks to establish, and what it bears on (the investigation’s setup or the incident). The link between declarations and tool calls can be read in two places, which belong to different process instances. In the original trace, the chain of thought and the tool calls are given together. In the investigation, the answers to the questions come from new samples of the model. On the side of actions, I will also distinguish the call issued by the model, its possible execution and its effects.

06Philosophical tools and envisaged corpus

This is a first corpus, still preliminary, that I have identified.

The programmatic article by Barton and Toyoshima (2026) seems to me particularly useful here to better define the system being evaluated (for example via the question of the bearer, which can be transposed to the measured tendencies). The authors distinguish at least five possible bearers of alignment, from the model itself to the sociotechnical system that combines the assistant and humans. A rerun first gives a frequency of outcomes. It will be necessary to say which disposition this frequency estimates the strength of, and what to attribute it to: the model alone, or the model together with the context it restarts from, a modified transcript or a summary depending on the investigation.

The dispositional reading, which I relate to the assignment of a probability to the triple (disposition, trigger, realization) in Barton et al. (2012), seems to me to correspond well to what a rerun estimates in the investigation’s setup: the strength of a tendency to produce an outcome in a given context. I intend to compare it with a Bayesian approach to probabilities, to see which is the more solid on this case and which is the more easily implementable. Finally, Barton and Toyoshima (2026) write that a system’s dispositions can change with the conversation history. When an investigation modifies the agent’s context, does it trigger the same disposition differently, or does it change it?

The other tools I am considering:

Causation
Interventionist, probabilistic and counterfactual approaches (Woodward 2003, Pearl 2000, Eronen 2020, Eells 1991, Lewis 1973, as well as Tian and Pearl 2000, Oberst and Sontag 2019): what the observed frequencies allow us to say about the original run.
Explanation
(Woodward 2003, Imbert 2013, Salmon 1971): knowing what makes a behaviour vary, is that explaining it or only knowing how to correct it?
Belief and self-report
(Borsboom et al. 2004, Nisbett and Wilson 1977, Toyoshima et al. 2020, Dennett 1987): what the system’s statements about itself are worth, and under what conditions a belief or an intention can be attributed to it.
Controls
Severity requirement of Mayo and Spanos 2006: which controls must accompany a result for it to count.
Experiment and simulation
(Barberousse et al. 2009, Parker 2020): under what conditions does the setup of a rerun, partly simulated, stand for the real incident?
Technical literature on AI safety
(Singh et al. 2026, Macar et al. 2025, Summerfield et al. 2025): what is already being done with methods close to reruns, and the limitations that have been identified in them.
