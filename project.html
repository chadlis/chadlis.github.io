Automated interpretability (autointerp) scores an explanation of a sparse autoencoder feature by asking a language model, the scorer, to predict from the explanation where the feature fires. Published pipelines usually fix one scorer. I ask whether the choice of scorer changes the judgments and the benefit of the explanation.

I hold texts and explanations fixed and compare five scorers (Claude Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Mistral Small 3.2) on 100 features of one SAE, 40 texts per feature with measured labels, two original explanations per feature and a placebo explanation taken from another feature (120,000 calls). Two scorers answer differently on 7 to 13% of the texts (Cohen’s kappa 0.72 to 0.84). Under the placebo every scorer falls to chance (48.6 to 49.5%), and the explanation brings each of them about 30 accuracy points. Four scorers gain a similar amount, with differences under one point, while Opus gains about 3 points more than each of them, because it answers “yes” more often: it finds more positives and raises more false alarms.

The scorers rank the 200 explanations alike (Spearman 0.76 to 0.90), but on 47 of the 100 features at least two of them prefer opposite explanations. A reading of 30 texts where the scorers disagree suggests that a large share of the disagreement comes from explanations that do not describe the feature exactly. The whole design was fixed and frozen before the first call.

Note added after submission. Two statements above are too strong. The higher “yes” rate of Opus explains at least part of its larger gain (section 03); its threshold and its use of the explanation are not separated here. And the scorer prompt was frozen on 19 November after format checks on 19 texts per scorer, which were real calls; texts, labels, placebos and the analysis plan were frozen before the pilot and the grid.

01The question

About this page. This is the web version of the extended report (11 pages) that I submitted to the Mechanistic Interpretability Hackathon 5: Autointerp, together with a three-page report. Both PDFs are linked above, unchanged. Where a statement has been made more precise since, the change is marked “Note added after submission”; the original text is kept.

A sparse autoencoder (SAE) rewrites the activations of a language model as a sum of many features, each of which fires on few tokens and is meant to stand for one idea. An LLM writes a short explanation of each feature from the texts where it fires most. To know whether the explanation is any good, the detection method of Paulo et al. (2024) shows the explanation and a text to a second LLM, the scorer, which answers 1 if it predicts that the feature fires on the text and 0 otherwise. The share of right answers is the score of the explanation.

The score therefore depends on two things: the explanation, which is what one wants to measure, and the scorer, which is a choice of the pipeline. Two recent works make this dependence a practical concern. First, van der Ben et al. (2026) show that the choices made in the pipeline move autointerp scores more than the choice of the SAE architecture. To isolate those choices, they keep a single scorer fixed (Gemini Flash), and they name the variation of the scorer as an axis of instability that they did not study. Second, Chen (2026) shows that on the stored scoring data of Neuronpedia for GPT-2 small, a simple rule that never reads the explanation (it answers “yes” when the central token of the excerpt is marked) reaches a higher balanced accuracy than the stored LLM scorers, and recommends reporting every score next to the best rule that does without the explanation. Taken together, these results raise two questions that a fixed-scorer study cannot answer: whether another scorer would give the same scores, and how much of a score actually comes from the explanation.

Scorers have been compared before. Paulo et al. (2024, appendix F.7) score the same explanations with three scorers (Claude Sonnet 3.5, Llama 70B and Llama 8B) and compare their median scores, and Chen (2026) compares simulators on identical inputs with original and shuffled explanations. To my knowledge (searches of 17 and 21 November 2026), no study measures, for several detection scorers, their agreement text by text, the gain each draws over an explanation-free control, and whether their disagreements change which explanation is preferred.

With texts and explanations held fixed, do scorer models differ in their judgments, and in the gain that the explanation brings them over an unrelated explanation?

I include Gemini Flash among the five scorers, so that my results can be related to the scorer that van der Ben et al. keep fixed. This study makes three contributions.

  1. It measures how often five detection scorers disagree on the same text, and not only whether their mean scores agree: two scorers answer differently on 7 to 13% of the texts.
  2. It measures, for each scorer and on exactly the same texts, the gain that the explanation brings over a placebo, so that the gains can be compared pair by pair: four scorers gain about the same, and Opus about 3 points more.
  3. In an exploratory analysis decided after the results, it asks whether these disagreements change which of two explanations of a feature a researcher would keep: two scorers pick opposite explanations on 13 to 27% of the features.

The question has two parts, and each part has its own measure, fixed before the first call. The first part is about judgments: do two scorers give the same answer on the same text? It is measured by the raw agreement and Cohen’s kappa of each pair of scorers (Figure 2). The second part is about the benefit of the explanation: the gain of a scorer is its accuracy with the original explanations minus its accuracy with the placebo. It is the central measure of the study (Figure 3). The placebo is what makes the gain meaningful: a scorer that answers well with the explanation could do so because of the explanation, or because of some cue in the texts themselves. Giving it the explanation of another feature, and nothing else changed, removes the first cause and keeps the second. The placebo is thus the explanation-free baseline that Chen (2026) recommends, measured for each scorer on exactly the same texts.

Note added after submission. A placebo is not an absence of explanation: the scorer still reads an explanation, of another feature. It plays the role of the explanation-free baseline recommended by Chen (2026) without being identical to it.

02Design

Figure 1 shows the chain. I describe each step in order. Appendix A gives the details, and PLAN.md in the repository gives every rule.

Features
The SAE is gpt2-small/6-res-jb (Bloom, 2024), read through the Neuronpedia API. The 24,576 features are shuffled once with a published seed (20261120) and examined in that order. A feature is kept if it fires on between 0.001% and 1% of tokens, has both original explanations (A, written by gpt-3.5-turbo, and B, by gpt-4o-mini), has at least 20 distinct stored passages where it fires, and passes a gate: its strongest stored window still fires when sent alone. The first 100 features kept form the sample. One of them later lost too many positives and was replaced by the next feature of the order.
Texts and labels
Each feature gets 40 texts of 17 GPT-2 tokens: 20 positives and 20 negatives. Every label is measured, never assumed: the feature’s activation is recomputed on the exact text shown to the scorer. A positive must reach at least 10% of the feature’s maximum activation, and the 20 positives are spread over the whole range, from the weakest to the strongest. A negative must have an activation of exactly 0. Negatives are windows stored for the other sampled features: positives and negatives then come from the same corpus, with the same length and the same cut, so that their form tells nothing about the label. In particular, no token is marked in the text shown to the scorer, and positives and negatives alike are windows centred on a token where some feature fires (position 8 of 17 for most of them), so the shortcut found by Chen (2026) is not available here.
Explanations and placebo
Each text is scored under three descriptions: explanation A, explanation B, and a placebo, which is the explanation of another feature. The placebos were chosen by a seeded assignment with filters (no shared content word, no feature close in the SAE’s decoder space, a comparable length, no generic description such as “punctuation”), then triaged by a fresh LLM agent and reviewed by me, in 14 rounds, until no pair was in doubt. Note added after submission. My review covered the doubtful pairs in some rounds only, not in all 14, and the last round left one light doubt, which I kept (Appendix A).
Scorers
Five scorers answer: Claude Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 (through Anthropic’s Message Batches API), and Gemini 2.5 Flash and Mistral Small 3.2 (through OpenRouter, with the provider pinned). Each call shows one text and one description, with the detection prompt of the delphi library (Paulo et al., 2024). The settings are the closest to deterministic that each model accepts (Appendix A, Table 3). Every call is made twice.
Volume and order of work
100 features, 40 texts, 3 descriptions, 5 scorers and 2 passes give 120,000 calls. A pilot on 5 features (6,000 calls, 1.39 USD) checked the whole chain against criteria fixed beforehand, then the grid ran (120,000 calls, 27.55 USD). Accuracies and gains use the mean of the two passes, agreement uses pass 1. Every interval is a 95% percentile interval from a bootstrap over features (10,000 resamples): the 40 texts of a feature share its explanation, so their errors are not independent, and the feature is the unit that is resampled.

03Results

Judgments: two scorers differ on 7 to 13% of the texts

Figure 2 gives the agreement of each of the 10 pairs. The raw agreement goes from 86.9% (Mistral and Opus) to 92.6% (Haiku and Sonnet): two scorers answer differently on 7 to 13% of the texts. Cohen’s kappa, which removes the agreement that two scorers would reach by chance given how often each says “yes”, goes from 0.72 to 0.84. The pairs are not all alike: the intervals of the two extreme pairs do not overlap ([0.68, 0.76] against [0.81, 0.86]). Pass 2 gives the same kappas within 0.01. These disagreements are much larger than the noise of a single scorer: each scorer changes its own answer between its two passes on only 0.1 to 3.1% of the texts. This stability is not a ceiling on the agreement between scorers, but it shows that the disagreements are not the result of sampling noise.

Gain: about 30 points for every scorer, 3 more for Opus

The placebo works as a control. Under the placebo, every scorer falls to an accuracy of 48.6 to 49.5%, that is to chance: the texts carry no cue of their label that a scorer could use without the explanation. (The values sit slightly below 50% because an unreadable answer counts as wrong.) With the explanations, the accuracy rises to 77 to 83%.

Figure 3a gives the gain of each scorer. It is about 30 points for every scorer, from 29.2 (Mistral) to 32.4 (Opus), and explanation B brings slightly more than explanation A to every scorer. Figure 3b gives the paired differences. Six of the 10 differences are compatible with 0, between −0.1 and +0.5 points, with intervals of about ±1.2 points: Haiku, Sonnet, Gemini and Mistral draw a similar benefit from the explanation. This is not a test of equivalence, since no equivalence margin was set in advance, and with simultaneous intervals (below) these six reach ±2.3 points. The four other differences are those of Opus, which gains about 3 points more than each of the four others (for example Opus minus Mistral: +3.2 points, interval [1.8, 4.6]).

The ten comparisons were fixed beforehand, and with ten comparisons about one in two series would exclude 0 once by chance. Here the four exclusions form a single pattern, Opus against every other scorer, rather than isolated exclusions. As a robustness check suggested by an external review after the results (DEVIATIONS.md, entry 3), I also computed Bonferroni intervals, each at 99.5%, so that the 10 hold together at 95%. The four differences of Opus still exclude 0 (from [0.7, 4.9] for Opus minus Sonnet to [1.2, 5.2] for Opus minus Mistral).

Where the differences come from

Table 1 and Figure 4 decompose these results. Because each feature has as many positives as negatives, the accuracy is the mean of the sensitivity and the specificity, and the gain splits into two parts:

gain = ½ (sensAB − sensP) + ½ (specAB − specP)

The first part counts the positives that the explanation lets the scorer recognise, the second the negatives it rejects better. For every scorer, the first part makes the gain (30.5 to 35.5 points) and the second part is a small loss (0.8 to 3.1 points): with the explanation, a scorer finds many more positives and raises a few more false alarms. Opus is the clearest case. With the explanations, it recognises 73% of the positives against 62 to 65% for the others, and answers “yes” to about 10% of the negatives against 4 to 7%. Its larger gain is therefore the balance of more positives found and more false alarms, and the first effect is larger.

Note added after submission. Recomputed from results.json, the false-alarm rate of the other scorers ranges from 3.8 to 8.5% (4 to 8% when A and B are averaged), not 4 to 7%; Opus is at 9.9 to 10.0%.

Interpretation. The difference between scorers on the gain thus appears to come, at least in part, from a different threshold for answering “yes”, and not only from a different understanding of the explanation.

The errors also depend on the strength of the positive. In the pilot, the scorers recognised about half of the weakest positives and nine in ten of the strongest (Appendix B). An explanation describes what makes a feature fire most, and the weakest activations are those it covers least.

Reading the texts where the scorers disagree

On pass 1 and with the explanations, the five scorers are not unanimous on 1,568 of 7,752 texts (20%). I drew 30 of them with a seed fixed beforehand, 15 positives and 15 negatives, and asked two fresh LLM agents (Claude Fable 5.2, with no other context and with the scorers renamed S1 to S5) to say for each text whether it fits the explanation and why the scorers split. The agents saw the labels and the activations, so their causes are hypotheses to test, for instance by a human annotation blind to the votes and the activations, rather than measurements. The two readings give the same cause for 25 of the 30 texts (kappa 0.79). (Note added after submission: as both readings come from the same model, their agreement shows that the reading is stable, not that it is right.) I also read the 30 texts myself, before seeing the agents’ readings but with the scorer names visible. My reading broadly agrees with theirs, but I did not record it text by text, so this agreement is not quantified.

Figure 5 gives the causes. On the positives, the disagreement has two forms. Either one or two scorers miss a cue that is present, or the majority answers 0 while the feature fires strongly, because the explanation, read by its meaning, does not cover what makes the feature fire. For example, a feature explained as “references to radio” fires on “Radio” in “Radioactive Man”: the scorer that answers 1 is right against the majority. On the negatives, a scorer often answers 1 because the text does contain what a too broad explanation describes, while the feature stays at 0. Across the 30 texts, the minority is right only 8 times. Both readings attribute 18 to 21 of the 30 disagreements to the explanation or the text rather than to a scorer error. The blind readings also recover the profiles of the table: the scorer that is most often alone in saying “yes” is Opus, and the two scorers that are alone only in saying “no” are Gemini and Sonnet.

Interpretation. A large share of the disagreement between scorers therefore seems to come from explanations that do not describe the feature exactly, which leaves each scorer to choose between a literal reading and a reading by meaning.

Exploratory: do scorers fail on the same texts? This analysis was added after the plan was frozen, before any grid result was read (DEVIATIONS.md, entry 1). For each pair of scorers, I compare the share of texts where both are wrong with the share expected if their errors were independent. Every pair fails together 3.4 to 4.1 times more often than independence would give (Figure 6 in Appendix B). This is expected: some texts, such as the weakest positives, are hard for every scorer. The three pairs within Claude have the highest ratios, and the mean ratio within Claude exceeds the mean across families by 0.37 (interval [0.23, 0.52]). This comparison rests on 3 pairs against 7, and family is mixed with the settings of the models, so it does not show an effect of the family as such.

Do scorers pick the same explanation? (exploratory)

This analysis was decided after the results (DEVIATIONS.md, entry 2), and an external review of the repository computed it first with the same definition. For each scorer, each of the 200 explanations (100 features, A and B) receives its accuracy on the 40 texts of its feature. Table 2 gives, for each pair of scorers, the rank correlation of these 200 accuracies, and how often the two scorers pick the same explanation of a feature, the opposite one, or a tie for at least one of them.

The scorers rank the explanations alike: the Spearman correlation goes from 0.76 (Opus and Mistral) to 0.90 (Haiku and Sonnet). Choosing between the two explanations of a feature is less stable. Two scorers pick opposite explanations on 13% (Haiku and Sonnet) to 27% (Opus and Gemini) of the features, and on 47 of the 100 features at least two of the five scorers prefer opposite explanations. Even Haiku and Mistral, whose mean gains differ by 0.4 points, make 20 opposite choices out of 100. To read these numbers, I compared each scorer with itself, pass 1 against pass 2: the opposite choices are then Haiku 6, Sonnet 1, Opus 7, Gemini 0, Mistral 0 out of 100. The reversals between scorers are thus well above the noise of a single scorer. They rest, however, on the 40 texts of each feature, and whether they hold on new texts is not tested here.

04Discussion

In this section, I discuss two questions that the results raise for the use of autointerp scores.

The first question is whether the choice of scorer matters. At the level of a single text, it does: two scorers disagree on 7 to 13% of the texts, which is well above the noise of a single scorer. At the level of the average gain, it matters much less: four of the five scorers draw a similar benefit from the explanation, and the fifth draws about 3 points more on a gain of 30. At the level of the choice between two explanations of a feature, it matters again: the scorers rank the 200 explanations alike, but two scorers pick opposite explanations on 13 to 27% of the features. Van der Ben et al. (2026) report that the ranking of the best-explained features can shift with the pipeline even when mean scores look stable, and my ranking analysis shows the same pattern for the scorer: a stable mean gain does not guarantee a stable choice of explanation.

The second question is what a scorer’s error measures. The reading of the divergent texts suggests that many errors are errors of the explanation rather than of the scorer: when an explanation is too narrow or too broad, a scorer that follows it faithfully is wrong against the measured label. The detection score of an explanation thus mixes the quality of the explanation with the threshold of the scorer, and this protocol does not separate the two.

05Conclusion

I can thus conclude that, on this SAE and with this prompt, the five scorers draw a similar average benefit from an explanation, within about 3 points, while two of them disagree on about one text in ten. The placebo control shows that the benefit is real, since every scorer falls to chance without the explanation. The larger gain of Opus comes with more false alarms, and a large share of the disagreement appears to come from the explanations themselves. The scorers rank explanations alike overall, but on almost half of the features at least two of them would retain a different one of the two explanations.

Note added after submission. “Chance” means an accuracy of about 50% on balanced texts. Under the placebo the scorers do not guess: they answer 0 to almost every text (sensitivity 1 to 2%, specificity 96 to 98%). This shows that they did not recognise the positives without the matching explanation; it does not rule out a cue in the texts usable with no explanation at all, an arm that was planned as an extension and not run.

Next steps

These directions need new scorer calls and are outside the frozen protocol.

  1. Score new texts for the same explanations, comparing one scorer on two disjoint sets of texts with two scorers on the same set, to tell whether the reversals of preference come from the scorer or from the choice of texts.
  2. Test explanations that are plausible but imperfect (too broad or too narrow, with semantically close negatives), to see whether the score rewards the precision of an explanation.
  3. Compare scorers at a fixed rate of false alarms, to separate a more permissive threshold from a better discrimination.

—What these results do not show

High agreement is not validity: scorers can be wrong together, and the exploratory analysis shows that they often are. A gain over the placebo shows that the explanation helps to predict where the feature fires, not that it describes a mechanism.

The study covers one SAE, one layer of a small model and 100 features, so nothing generalises to other SAEs. It uses five scorers, three of them from the same family, and one prompt. The settings differ from one scorer to another (thinking switched off, minimal or not available), so a difference between two scorers mixes the model and its setting. The positives are taken from the stored examples, which the explaining model may have seen, so they are not a sample of the natural distribution of the feature. One text is shown per call, which is not delphi’s batched protocol. Finally, nothing reliable can be said about whether explanation A or B is better for a given feature.

ADetails of the design

Draw
The features are examined in a list shuffled with seed 20261120. Of the first 114 examined, 5 failed the frequency filter, 8 failed the gate, and one (21484, “ISBN numbers”) kept only 16 positives once the activations were recomputed on the 17-token texts, and was replaced by the next eligible feature (22836, “references to FIFA”). The sample holds 1 feature that fires on 0.001 to 0.01% of tokens, 72 on 0.01 to 0.1% and 27 on 0.1 to 1%.
Texts
A positive passage is a stored window where the feature fires, and a passage is counted once even when Neuronpedia stores several sliding windows of it. The 20 positives are taken at evenly spaced ranks of the recomputed activation. The negatives are drawn, with a seed, from the stored windows of the other sampled features, never from the feature whose explanation is the placebo, each passage used once. Labels were recomputed with Neuronpedia’s activation API on the exact text shown to the scorer (line breaks replaced by spaces). Over the 100 features, 170 candidate positives fell below the threshold and were set aside, and 91 candidate negatives had a non-zero activation and were replaced. The document separator token appears in 9.7% of positives and 8.6% of negatives, and the mean length is 16.40 and 16.39 GPT-2 tokens.
Placebos
The final assignment uses 100 distinct donors, 21 of them outside the sample, 50 explanations A and 50 explanations B, and no feature is its own donor. Seven placebo texts were forbidden along the rounds. The last round left one light doubt, which I kept.
Scoring
The prompt is delphi’s detection prompt (commit a11130d), with four changes: the three solved examples are removed, one sentence is reworded, and two sentences are added on the format of the answer and on excerpts cut mid-sentence. The answer is read with delphi’s parser. An unreadable answer is retried once, then counted as invalid: wrong for accuracy, left out of agreement.
Pilot and grid
The pilot (5 features, 6,000 calls, 1.39 USD) had to show placebo accuracy between 40 and 60% and less than 2% invalid answers for every scorer, the real cost per call, and both central measures computed end to end. It passed. No scorer changed its answer on more than 5% of calls, so no third pass was added. The grid (120,000 calls) cost 27.55 USD: 25.77 USD for the Claude batches and 1.78 USD through OpenRouter. One call failed on a transport error and was resumed. In all, 739 answers (0.6%) were invalid after their retry.
Changes after the freeze
DEVIATIONS.md lists three: the exploratory analysis of shared errors, decided before any grid result was read (entry 1); the ranking analysis, decided after the results (entry 2); and the Bonferroni intervals for the gain differences, a robustness check suggested after the results (entry 3). (Updated after submission; the submitted version listed only the first two.)
